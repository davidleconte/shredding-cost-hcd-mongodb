One field, five operations: how physical storage layout predicts write cost, index freshness and concurrency in document databases

A comparison of the IBM DataStax HCD 2.0 Data API and MongoDB 8.x, with Apache Cassandra 6.0 and the Accord protocol in view — and an explicit account, claim by claim, of what is documented, what is reported and what remains unverified

David Leconte — Customer Success Engineer, IBM France | Software, Data & AI | Horizon
Roughly eighteen minutes to read. The three exercises at the end take an afternoon between them.

Abstract

Two document databases that expose near-identical interfaces — JSON in, JSON out — diverge sharply in their write-path behaviour. This paper argues that the divergence is not incidental but derivable: it follows from one decision about how a document is laid out on disk, and that decision predicts four observable properties that practitioners usually meet one surprise at a time. Those properties are the marginal cost of a single-field mutation, the data-modelling regime the engine rewards, the interval between a write completing and the corresponding index entry becoming visible, and the interpretability of any published benchmark.

The third property deserves particular emphasis and receives it here. For systems performing retrieval-augmented generation, the write-to-searchable interval is not a performance characteristic to be optimised opportunistically. It is a correctness property, and belongs in acceptance criteria rather than in a dashboard.

The paper closes by examining the fifth and least-discussed of the five operations — the consensus round that guards a conditional write — and its designated successor in Apache Cassandra, the Accord protocol specified in CEP-15. The analysis finds that Accord modifies the arbitration of a write while leaving its decomposition untouched, a result that strengthens rather than weakens the central thesis.

A note on epistemic status. Comparative writing about competing databases fails most often not through malice but through the silent promotion of plausible mechanism to documented fact. Every substantive claim below therefore carries one of four markers, and the appendix records the exact passage on which each rests.

D Documented. Established by the vendor's own version-pinned documentation, by the project's source of record, or by peer-reviewed publication. Quoted verbatim in the appendix.

R Reported. Described publicly by the engineers who built the system, or by the upstream project, but not restated in the versioned documentation of the shipped product. Directionally reliable; not a contractual guarantee.

U Unverified. Asserted here on structural grounds, or widely repeated in the practitioner literature, but not established by any source of acceptable rank that I was able to consult. Treat as a hypothesis to be tested against your own build.

M Measured. Observed on a named build under stated conditions, by a harness reproduced in the appendix. Stronger than U, weaker than D: it establishes what one build did on one day, not what the vendor commits to. Every M claim names its measurement (M1–M4) and every measurement names its conditions.

Nota bene on the figures. A diagram carries more apparent authority than a paragraph: it resembles an architecture specification even when it is an argument. Every figure below therefore declares in its caption which of its elements are documented and which are not. Where a figure depicts something marked U, the caption says so, and the reader should read the box as a hypothesis drawn in rectangles rather than as a contract.

1. The phenomenon

A pattern recurs in design reviews with sufficient regularity to warrant formal treatment.

A team ships a service backed by a document store. Initial performance is satisfactory. Over some months the document schema accretes: a status enumeration, two or three timestamps, a nested object carrying audit provenance. Read latency remains stable. Write latency degrades — and degrades disproportionately to the additional bytes committed. Because no one has altered the write pattern, no one examines the write path. The investigation goes to the application tier, to the network, to garbage collection, and returns empty-handed.

The explanation is neither a defect, nor a tuning deficiency, nor a regression introduced by a release. It is the deterministic consequence of a decision taken long before the team arrived: how the engine physically represents a document on disk. Recover that decision and the remainder of the system becomes predictable rather than surprising — including behaviours with no apparent connection to writes at all, such as the latency with which a newly written document becomes retrievable.

The argument is developed through two systems that resolve the same design question in opposite directions: the JSON document interface layered over a Cassandra-derived engine (the IBM DataStax HCD 2.0 Data API5) and MongoDB 8.x.

2. Two resolutions of the decomposition problem

2.1 Decomposition: one document, one row, many derived columns

The intuitive model holds that a document database stores documents. Some do. This one does something considerably more interesting, and the difference is the hinge of everything that follows.

R A JSON document submitted to the Data API is shredded: decomposed into a single database row bearing a generic column layout that captures, separately, the original document text, the set of paths present within it, typed value maps for each JSON scalar type, array membership and array cardinality.11 Indexes are then constructed over the filterable columns.

M The specific column names commonly cited in the practitioner literature — doc_json, exist_keys, array_contains, array_size, and the several query_*_values maps — are widely repeated but do not appear, so far as I could establish, in any version-pinned DataStax documentation or in a readable source file of the upstream project. I reproduce them below because they are useful as a mental model and because naming them makes the claim falsifiable. They should not be treated as an interface. On HCD 2.0.6 with Data API v1.0.33 (appendix, measurement M1) all nine appear under exactly these names, alongside three that the practitioner literature omits: key, the typed partition key; tx_id, a timeuuid that guards every conditional write; and query_lexical_value, the lexical column. Nine storage-attached indexes were created on that collection, which declared no vector option. That is one build's answer, not an interface; verify against the build you deploy.

D The benefit of decomposition is real, immediate and easily underestimated. Any field becomes filterable without anyone declaring an index for it. There is no migration to add a predicate, no ticket to a database administrator to render a new attribute searchable. The upstream project's configuration record fixes the arithmetic: ten indexes are required per collection, against fifty available per database by default1 — five collections before exhaustion, on the upstream defaults. (That figure is the budget the API checks at creation; the collection measured in the appendix, created without a vector option, received nine — measurement M1.) The shipped HCD product raises the ceiling through a guardrail set to one hundred total storage-attached indexes.3 For a team iterating rapidly on an evolving schema, this is a genuine accelerant.

Everything that follows is the invoice for it.

2.2 In-place mutation

D MongoDB resolves the question in the opposite direction, and its documentation is explicit on all three components of that resolution.

Atomicity. "In MongoDB, write operations are atomic on the single-document level, even if modifying multiple values."34 The guarantee is bounded at the document, and the documentation is equally explicit that it stops there: when a single operation modifies several documents, each modification is atomic but the operation as a whole is not.34 This is a narrower guarantee than the phrase "atomic update" suggests to a reader arriving from a relational background, and the narrowness matters.

Absence of full rewrite. The $set reference states the advantage in comparative terms against full document replacement: "Targeted Updates: $set modifies only the specified fields, ensuring efficient updates by avoiding unnecessary writes and overhead when you work with large documents."35 The same page notes the replication corollary, that $set writes only the updated fields to the oplog. The contrast with decomposition is therefore not an inference drawn by this article; it is the distinction the vendor itself draws, between its operator and its own replacement path.

Declared indexes only. MongoDB creates exactly one index without being asked — a unique index on _id, at collection creation, which cannot be dropped.36 Every other index is created explicitly. The write therefore maintains what the schema designer decided it should maintain, and nothing else.

D One qualification is owed here, and omitting it would make the comparison less honest than it should be. MongoDB does provide an opt-in analogue of automatic indexing: the wildcard index, declared over the pattern $**, which "can support queries on any arbitrary field within documents in the collection."37 The distinction between the two platforms on this axis is consequently not capability but default. One indexes broadly unless instructed otherwise; the other indexes nothing unless instructed. That is a meaningful difference — defaults govern the systems nobody revisited — but it is a smaller and more interesting difference than "automatic versus manual", which is how the comparison is usually and lazily framed.

3. The cost function of a single-field mutation

Because the document has been taken apart, changing one field cannot be a targeted write. It becomes a sequence:

  1. Read the entire document into memory.
  2. Modify it there.
  3. Rewrite the whole document, together with every derived column.
  4. Maintain the indexes — on the build measured here, over every indexed field of the document, not only the fields that changed (appendix, measurement M3).
  5. Guard the write-back with a consensus-based conditional operation, so that a concurrent writer cannot silently overwrite it.

M Steps one through three describe a read-modify-write cycle. This is the structural inference on which the article's title rests, and I must be candid about its evidentiary basis: it follows prima facie from decomposition — a derived column cannot be recomputed without the document that derives it — but I could not find it asserted in any DataStax documentation for the current Data API. The one official statement on the subject that I could locate concerns the earlier, architecturally distinct Documents API, and it says the opposite, that the engineers resolved to avoid read-before-write at all costs.12 Pace that 2020 statement, which concerns a different API generation and cannot settle the question either way. What settles it, for a build you control, is a query trace rather than a latency curve. On HCD 2.0.6 with Data API v1.0.33, with tracing enabled on the node, every updateOne carrying a single $set produced two CQL statements: a SELECT of key, tx_id and doc_json by key, then one UPDATE rewriting every derived column and the whole doc_json, guarded by IF tx_id = ? (appendix, measurement M2). The cycle is present on that build. A cost-versus-size probe on the same build grew in step with a same-size read and could not, on its own, separate a read from a read-plus-rewrite; it is the trace, not the curve, that carries the claim.

D Step five is on firmer ground. The upstream configuration record exposes a client-side retry count for lightweight-transaction failure, defaulting to three.1 The Paxos machinery underlying conditional writes in this engine family is documented explicitly: a prepare phase in which a proposer circulates a proposal number to the replicas of a key, promises returned by a quorum of acceptors, then a proposal of the value, and so forth.13 The consistency levels at which this executes in the Data API are M not published by any source I consulted; measured on HCD 2.0.6 with Data API v1.0.33, every statement on the collection ran at LOCAL_QUORUM and every conditional statement carried LOCAL_SERIAL as its serial level (appendix, measurement M4). Earlier drafts asserted LOCAL_SERIAL from memory; it is restored here on the strength of a trace, on one node with RF = 1 — the name is established, the multi-replica cost is not.

The net effect — two CQL statements for an update and for a delete, one for an insert, on the build measured (M2) — is a write materially heavier than a blind CQL insertion, and — importantly — one that is not idempotent in the way a blind insertion is. Neither this nor MongoDB's single-step alternative is a defect. Both are the determinate consequence of a storage decision.

3.1 Two habits that follow directly

The difference surfaces in two places under your control: how you write, and what identifier you assign the record.

# astrapy — the Python client for the Data API
#
# (1) Server-generated identifier: convenient, and NOT safe to retry.
# If the network fails after the write applied, a retry creates a second document.
collection.insert_one({"payer": "acct-42", "amount_cents": 125000})

# (2) Identifier derived from the business key: a retry is rejected as a duplicate,
# so the operation becomes naturally idempotent. Same cost, far better failure mode.
collection.insert_one({
    "_id": "pmt-2026-08-07-000123",   # from the payment reference, not random
    "payer": "acct-42",
    "amount_cents": 125000,
})

That single choice eliminates an entire category of production incident, on either platform, at no runtime cost. It is the cheapest correctness improvement available in this class of system.

The second habit is to let the engine apply the change rather than performing it yourself:

# Anti-pattern: read-modify-write in application code.
# Three round trips, and two concurrent workers can silently lose one update.
doc = collection.find_one({"_id": "pmt-...000123"})
doc["status"] = "SETTLED"
collection.replace_one({"_id": doc["_id"]}, doc)   # rewrites everything you read

# Better: transmit the intent, not the document.
# One round trip. Whatever the engine does internally, it does it once,
# atomically, and without shipping the document across the wire twice.
collection.update_one(
    {"_id": "pmt-2026-08-07-000123"},
    {"$set": {"status": "SETTLED"}},
)

The opening phenomenon now resolves itself. Write latency crept upward because the team added indexed content — each new field was silently enrolled in the maintained index set, and it is the volume of that indexed content, not the number of fields, that the write pays for (appendix, measurement M11): at a fixed indexed volume, multiplying the field count fourteenfold barely moved the cost, while at a fixed field count the cost rose almost linearly with the bytes indexed. Nothing about their write pattern changed. The cost of their write pattern did.

M Where that cost sits is itself a design fact the paragraph above leaves implicit. Measured on this build (appendix, measurement M12), the per-byte rate splits roughly seventy–thirty between the two components that both grow with indexed bytes: about seventy per cent is the read, the conditional rewrite and the index maintenance, in the stateful storage engine that scales only by adding data-bearing nodes; the remaining quarter to a third is the JSON parse, the shred and the response serialisation, in the stateless Data API tier that scales by adding instances behind a load balancer. On a latency chart they are the same milliseconds, but they are not the same object of design — the minority is elastic, the majority is not. Two independent methods placed the split in that neighbourhood yet disagreed by more than a quarter at the largest sizes, so read it as a direction, not a precise ratio.

4. What decomposition purchases

Three properties, of which the third is the one comparative treatments most consistently omit.

Queryability without index design. Already treated, and genuinely valuable early in a product's life, when the schema is still an argument rather than a contract.

Write scale-out without a coordinating primary. No single node must accept every write, so capacity is added by adding nodes rather than by enlarging one.

An index current at the moment the write completes. D Because indexing occurs on the write path, a document becomes searchable as soon as it is written. The HCD 2.0 documentation states this directly, and attributes it to the vector index implementation by name:

"When you insert data, JVector adds those new documents to the graph immediately, so you can efficiently search right away."

DataStax, About vector databases, Hyper-Converged Database 2.0.4

M One qualifier the vendor sentence omits, and it only holds above a single replica. At RF > 1 the Data API reads a vector search at LOCAL_ONE — one replica — while it commits a write at LOCAL_QUORUM — two of three. "Searchable as soon as it is written" is therefore a single-replica read guarantee, not a quorum one: a vector query issued at acknowledgement can, in principle, read the one replica the write has not yet reached. Measured on a co-located three-replica test ring the effect was not detectable — in 120 of 120 cycles the just-written vector was the top match on the first LOCAL_ONE query, because sub-millisecond inter-replica latency closes the gap far below the round trip that carries the query. The window is bounded below the measurement floor there; a wide-area deployment, where inter-replica latency is tens of milliseconds, is exactly where it would surface — the same window this article attributes to the other engine in Figure 2. The searchability guarantee is real; its scope is one replica, and the reader deploying across datacentres should measure it there.

4.1 Index freshness as a correctness property

This third point changes character entirely depending on what is being built, and the change is categorical rather than one of degree.

For a product catalogue, several seconds of index lag is invisible; no user experiences it and no requirement references it. For a retrieval-augmented application it is not a performance detail at all. An assistant that records a fact and cannot retrieve it on the following turn is producing wrong answers — not slow answers, wrong ones — however promptly the index subsequently converges. The failure is semantic, it is invisible to latency instrumentation, and it will be reported by users as hallucination rather than as staleness. A fortiori, any system that writes and reads within a single conversational turn inherits the interval as a hard correctness bound rather than as a service objective.

If you are building a system of that kind, the write-to-searchable interval belongs in your acceptance criteria, not in your performance backlog.

4.2 The opposite resolution, stated in the vendor's own terms

D MongoDB's self-managed search and vector search execute in a separate process, mongot, running alongside the database.1516 MongoDB describes the mechanism precisely:

"mongot builds and maintains these compute-intensive indexes outside of the transaction commit window by leveraging change streams to asynchronously replicate changes to source data, allowing customers to add search and vector search capabilities without fear of impacting existing transactional workloads."

MongoDB, Now Source Available: The Engine Powering MongoDB Search, 15 January 2026.14

This is a deliberate and defensible design. It removes index maintenance from the write path, which is precisely the cost the decomposing engine elects to pay. Two coherent answers to one question, and the trade is legible in a single sentence from each vendor — which is a considerably better basis for a decision than any benchmark.

Note that the two designs are not in competition on a single axis. They optimise different things and the choice is properly determined by the workload, not by preference: ceteris paribus, a catalogue tolerates the window and profits from the throughput; a retrieval system does not tolerate the window at all.

D One corollary follows from the quoted mechanism and requires no further source: asynchronous replication of changes implies a non-zero interval between a write committing and the corresponding search index reflecting it. U What that interval is, and whether MongoDB documents any bound on it, I did not establish. This is again a measurement question rather than a reading question.

D Status matters here and moves quickly. As of the announcement of 15 January 2026, mongot entered public preview under the Server Side Public License.14 U Contemporaneous MongoDB material describes general availability as anticipated following the preview; I was unable to confirm the current status per edition at the time of writing, and I therefore make no claim about it. Consult the release notes for the edition you intend to deploy. This article is a pointer to those notes, never a substitute for them.

5. What decomposition costs, and the design rule that follows

The cost localises in one identifiable regime: workloads that repeatedly mutate the same record. Counters. Status machines transiting a sequence of states. Hot aggregates updated on every event. There, the in-place update is structurally cheaper, and no quantity of tuning alters the structure.

Model for append and read at scale, not for repeated mutation of the same record.

If your access pattern genuinely concentrates writes on individual records — and some legitimately do — that is valuable information about which engine suits that component. It is far cheaper to learn in week one than in month six, and cheaper still to learn from a storage diagram than from an incident review.

D It bears stating explicitly, since the term "transaction" recurs throughout this literature with three mutually incompatible senses, that the DataStax documentation is unambiguous about the guarantees on offer sensu stricto: HCD does not adhere to ACID transactions with rollback or locking mechanisms, and a write operation is atomic at the partition level.33 Section 8 examines what is changing upstream on exactly this point.

6. Three design rules

6.1 Keep partitions bounded, and know the numbers

D A brief detour, because it matters here. The platform offers two data models side by side: schemaless collections, which is what everything above has described, and schema-defined tables carrying an explicit primary key.6 Collections suit documents whose shape varies; tables suit records of stable shape read by key and range — ledgers, event trails, time series. Both are reached through the same JSON API; neither requires dropping into a query language.7

D Where tables are used, the published sizing guidance is explicit and arithmetically actionable: keep rows per partition under one hundred thousand and partition size under one hundred megabytes.8 The corroborating modelling guidance states the second bound independently.9 Note that the documentation specifies rows; a document decomposed to one row makes the two coincide here, but the distinction survives elsewhere and is worth preserving in your own notes.

The partition key determines how rows are grouped, and a key that accumulates without bound breaches both limits eventually:

// A table's primary key is declared in JSON, as part of createTable.
// partitionBy   -> how rows are grouped and distributed
// partitionSort -> the ordering within a partition (1 ascending, -1 descending)

// UNBOUNDED: one partition per account, growing for the life of the account.
// Flawless in test. Degrades reads, repairs and streaming in year two.
"primaryKey": {
  "partitionBy": ["account_id"],
  "partitionSort": { "event_ts": -1 }
}

// BOUNDED: the month joins the partition key, so each partition covers a
// fixed window. A query spanning months reads a handful of partitions
// rather than one enormous one.
"primaryKey": {
  "partitionBy": ["account_id", "yyyymm"],
  "partitionSort": { "event_ts": -1 }
}

Choose the window from your own arithmetic: the busiest account's events per month, multiplied by bytes per event, held beneath both bounds above.10 This single decision forestalls the majority of the difficulties popularly attributed to this class of database.

6.2 Choose what gets indexed, deliberately

Automatic indexing is a default, not a law. If a field will demonstrably never be filtered upon, excluding it renders every write to that document cheaper. On the other platform the same discipline applies mutatis mutandis, inverted in its direction: declare the indexes you need, and no more. In both cases the discipline is the same act — deciding, rather than inheriting.

6.3 Measure write-to-searchable before designing around it

It is twelve lines, and virtually nobody runs it:

import time, uuid

marker = f"probe-{uuid.uuid4()}"        # unique, so nothing else can match it
t0 = time.perf_counter()
collection.insert_one({"_id": marker, "body": marker})

while True:                              # poll until the write is findable
    if collection.find_one({"body": marker}):
        print(f"write-to-searchable: {(time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000:.0f} ms")
        break
    time.sleep(0.01)

Run it idle, then run it again under write load. This probe measures index freshness, not the write path; it does not tell you whether an update reads before it writes. For that, enable query tracing on one node for a handful of updates and read system_traces — the method behind measurement M2. The second run is the one that matters, because it is under load that the two architectures diverge and under load that your users will encounter whichever behaviour you inherited. A quarter of an hour establishes whether index freshness is a constraint you must design around or one you may safely ignore. If you are building anything with retrieval in it, this is the highest-yield fifteen minutes in this article.

7. Where JVector actually appears — three loci, three statuses

A persistent conflation in this area will invalidate an evaluation if carried into it. The same library appears in three distinct places with three distinct statuses, and one place where it does not appear at all.

D In Apache Cassandra 5.0 upstream: it does not. Upstream vector search through storage-attached indexing was specified and built on Lucene's Hierarchical Navigable Small World implementation. The enhancement proposal is explicit: the implementation leverages Lucene's HNSW library, characterised there as the best available ANN algorithm for Java at the time.21 Storage-attached indexing itself is a 5.0 feature.22

D In the DataStax distribution: as the shipped engine. JVector — a graph index of the DiskANN family, supporting product and binary quantisation19 — is part of what the distribution adds atop the Cassandra 5.0 base,5 and the HCD documentation names it as the mechanism behind immediate searchability.4

D In OpenSearch: as an official plugin, not a core engine. The documented core k-NN engines are Lucene, Faiss and the deprecated NMSLIB, the latter superseded in favour of the first two;17 the default engine changed from NMSLIB to Faiss in the 2.18 line, and index creation on NMSLIB is blocked from OpenSearch 3.0.18 JVector is available separately as opensearch-project/opensearch-jvector, Apache-2.0 licensed and governed within the OpenSearch project, with a release train tracking OpenSearch versions.20 Its README claims thread-safe concurrent index construction, incremental merges in place of full graph rebuilds, and — directly relevant here — compatibility with Cassandra, "which allows to more easily transfer vector encoded data from Cassandra to OpenSearch and vice versa."19

The practical consequence: a benchmark of "Cassandra vector search" tells you nothing about the DataStax distribution, and a benchmark of either tells you nothing about the OpenSearch plugin. Three artefacts, three governance models, three release cadences. Establish which one your evaluation actually exercised before you read its numbers.

8. The fifth operation and its designated successor

The five-step account above assigns the final step to a consensus round. That step, alone among the five, has a named successor already in flight, and any reader holding a current view of the Cassandra roadmap will ask about it. The answer is more interesting than a simple replacement narrative, and it is worth developing with some care, because it both qualifies and reinforces the thesis of this article.

8.1 What Accord specifies

D CEP-15 proposes Accord, a leaderless timestamp protocol for general-purpose distributed transactions. Its stated goals are transactions operating over any set of keys atomically, strict-serializable isolation, one wide-area round trip under normal conditions, failure tolerance unaffected by any minority of replica failures, absence of any introduced bottleneck, and — the goal most relevant here — support for live migration from Paxos. Two techniques are proposed as novel: a reorder buffer guaranteeing single-round-trip consensus under a timestamp protocol, and fast path electorates preserving leaderless fast-path availability under worst tolerated failure. Timestamps are hybrid logical clocks made globally unique by appending each replica's identifier. The proposal situates itself in the EPaxos lineage, claiming better properties than Caesar or Tempo.23

The single round trip is a design goal inscribed in the proposal, not a measurement. U No benchmark figures published by the Apache Software Foundation were located. Practitioners will encounter a great many performance claims about Accord in secondary material; none that I could trace to a primary source.

8.2 Coexistence, not replacement

D This is the point most frequently mangled in summary. The proposal is explicit on both horizons:

"This work is expected to replace Paxos for the project. In order to support live migration both protocols will be maintained for at least an interim period, after which it is expected that Paxos will be deprecated. The precise timing of this will be up to the community."

CEP-15, General Purpose Transactions, section "Long Term".23

And, on the architecture of the coexistence: supporting the Paxos-based lightweight transaction and Accord side by side is cited in the proposal as an instance of the intended modularity.23 No deprecation of Paxos has been announced. Lightweight transactions remain the mechanism in the field.

8.3 Release status, stated precisely

D Apache Cassandra 6.0-alpha1 is tagged 3 April 2026 and 6.0-alpha2 is tagged 7 August 2026 in the project repository.24 The release announcement characterises the build without ambiguity: a quarterly alpha on the 6.0 series, and quarterly alphas are intended only for development and testing purposes.25

D The NEWS.txt of the 6.0-alpha2 tag enumerates, under the 6.0 new-features heading: JDK 21 with generational ZGC as default collector, CEP-37 Auto Repair, CEP-21 Transactional Cluster Metadata, CEP-42 Constraints Framework, CEP-24, CEP-43 and CEP-55, unified-compaction parallelisation, JMX configuration in YAML, and several new CQL functions. Accord is not among them. This absence is suggestive but not probative: the same file carries an explicit editorial placeholder, and its upgrade section still refers to the release as 5.1, evidencing a renumbering not yet reflected throughout.26

D The tracking epic for CEP-15 is titled "Accord Beta" and stands, at consultation, In Progress and Unresolved; the linked ticket for writing the CEP-15 documentation is likewise In Progress.27 R Documentation under review in the project describes the activation model: a global switch in cassandra.yaml (accord: enabled: true) and a per-table property (WITH transactional_mode = 'full'), under which ordinary CQL operations on that table execute through Accord.28 The explicit transaction surface — BEGIN TRANSACTION, LET, conditional block, COMMIT TRANSACTION — appears in project issue traffic.29 Alpha syntax, subject to change.

D U A qualification I owe the reader: I was unable to render the cassandra.yaml shipped at the 6.0-alpha2 tag, and therefore cannot assert from a primary source that the accord configuration block is present in that specific artefact. What is established is that Accord is absent from that tag's release notes, that its tracking epic is unresolved, and that where it is enabled it is enabled deliberately, per table, rather than by default.

8.4 What Accord changes in the five-operation model — and what it does not

Here the analysis becomes unusually clean, because the proposal settles it by its own terms. Among the explicit non-goals of CEP-15 are SASI, SAI and local secondary indexes.23 Storage-attached indexing — step four of our five — is outside the proposal's scope by declaration, not by inference.

StepBelongs toModified by Accord?
1 — Read the whole documentStorage modelNo
2 — Modify in memoryStorage modelNo
3 — Rewrite document and derived columnsStorage modelNo
4 — Maintain storage-attached indexesStorage modelNo — excluded by CEP-15's stated non-goals
5 — Guard with consensusConsensus mechanismYes, and this step alone

Accord alters how a write is arbitrated. It does not alter how a document is decomposed and rewritten. The thesis of this article is therefore not weakened by the arrival of a successor protocol; it is corroborated by it. A change of consensus mechanism — a genuine advance in distributed systems, formally specified and years in construction — leaves untouched the four steps that derive from physical layout. That is exactly what one should expect if the layout decision is the real predictor, and exactly what one should not expect if the five-step cost were an artefact of the consensus round. The successor protocol is, in this precise sense, a controlled experiment on the article's own hypothesis.

D One consequence for readers evaluating the commercial distribution: no mention of Accord appears in the HCD documentation consulted, and HCD 2.0 is based on Cassandra 5.0.5 Accord is therefore not available there today. Treat it as a reason to follow the 6.0 line attentively, not as a property of any system you can deploy to production this quarter.

9. On reading published limits — including this article's

This section began life as a methodological aside and earned promotion, because the error it describes is one I committed myself and did not catch until audit.

The upstream open-source project behind the Data API and the shipped commercial product do not carry identical limits, and reading the wrong one silently corrupts a design. D The upstream configuration record sets a default read page size of fifty and a maximum insert count of twenty per operation, alongside ten indexes required per collection and fifty available per database.1 The commercial limits documentation for the same API surface publishes a page size of twenty and a maximum of one hundred insertions per transaction.2 The product guardrail for total storage-attached indexes is one hundred.3

Observe that the divergence does not run in a single direction. Page size is lower in the product; insert volume is higher; the index ceiling is higher. An earlier draft of this article reported the insert batch as 100 upstream against 20 in the product — reversing the direction of the second row and misattributing the third. Three wrong numbers, produced by reading the upstream repository where the product documentation was called for, and reproduced with complete confidence until they were checked against the sources now listed in the appendix.

U I should add that I was unable to render the HCD 2.0 limits page itself during verification; the product figures above come from the equivalent commercial documentation for the same Data API surface, which is a source of adjacent rather than identical scope. The rule this yields is therefore one I am in a poor position to preach and a good position to demonstrate:

Verify the limits that constrain your design against the build you will actually deploy — not against a vendor's documentation for an adjacent edition, not against an upstream repository, not against a comparison article, and not against this one. It takes minutes, and it is the difference between a constraint known ex ante and one that finds you in production.

10. Methodological requirements for comparison

If you intend to compare these systems empirically, the distance between a defensible benchmark and a worthless one is six requirements, and most published comparisons violate at least three. None is my invention. The first two address coordinated omission, named by Gil Tene, whose wrk2 and HdrHistogram exist precisely to correct it: the tool combines constant-throughput load generation with latency measurement that accounts for the intended rate.30 The remainder address, inter alia, the documented weaknesses of the Yahoo! Cloud Serving Benchmark upon which most published NoSQL comparisons rest.31

  1. Drive load at a fixed offered rate, not as fast as the system replies. A loop that awaits each response conceals precisely the stalls under investigation and systematically understates the tail.
  2. Report the distribution, not the average. Median, p99, p99.9, maximum. Averages conceal the behaviour that pages you at three in the morning.
  3. Hold durability equivalent, and record the mapping. The two systems express durability differently; select a defensible equivalence, disclose it, and do not vary it between runs.
  4. State the working set against available memory, and execute both an in-cache and a disk-bound regime. A latency figure without this is uninterpretable, not merely incomplete.
  5. Declare the topology, including any coordinating tier. Throughput scales with what was provisioned, and a stateless API tier is a provisioning decision.
  6. Warm up, then run long enough to cross the background maintenance cycles. Compaction and checkpointing are steady state, not noise.

Apply all six to both systems, or the comparison characterises your test rig rather than the databases.

D For a peer-reviewed comparison of these two database families, the reference is Haughian, Osman and Knottenbelt, Benchmarking Replication in Cassandra and MongoDB NoSQL Datastores, DEXA 2016.32 U I recommend reading it for its stated limitations rather than its results — but I must withdraw the specific characterisation of those limitations that appeared in an earlier draft, since I was unable to obtain the full text and will not paraphrase a paper's methodology section from its abstract. Read the limitations yourself; that exercise is, in any case, more instructive than the numbers.

11. What to do this week

Two of the three apply whatever database you run today. None takes longer than an afternoon.

1 — Measure your write-to-searchable interval. Fifteen minutes.
Run the twelve-line probe from section 6.3 against whatever you already operate, once idle and once under write load. You will obtain a number you almost certainly do not currently have, and it is the number that determines whether index lag is a constraint you must design around. If you are building anything with retrieval in it, this is the highest-value quarter-hour available to you.

2 — Establish who generates your record identifiers. One hour.
Walk the writes that matter — payments, orders, anything on which a duplicate would be expensive — and determine whether the identifier derives from your business data or from the database. Convert the ones that matter. This eliminates a category of production incident, costs nothing at runtime, and behaves identically on every database in this class.

3 — Ask your own database how it stores a record. An afternoon.
Whatever you operate, establish whether it keeps a record whole or takes it apart, and what it indexes automatically. Then re-read your slowest write path in that light. In the majority of cases the explanation is already there, and the investigation has been examining the wrong layer.

12. Conclusion

The conclusion is not that one of these databases is superior. It is something more portable:

Establish how your database physically stores a record, and you can predict most of the rest — what an update genuinely costs, what you should model for, when your index is current, and where the system ceases to be the right answer.

That question is worth putting to any database under evaluation, and to the one already in production. It will save more time than a dozen feature comparison tables, and — as section 8 indicates — it remains the right question even as the consensus layer beneath it is replaced by a formally specified successor. Layout outlives protocol.

A second conclusion concerns method rather than architecture, and I would not have written it a year ago. Of the claims in this article, a substantial minority could not be established from sources of acceptable rank, including several that are universally repeated and that I personally believe to be true. Marking them as unverified rather than quietly asserting them makes this a less confident article. It also makes it a falsifiable one, which is the only kind worth writing about someone else's system while employed by their competitor.

What the measurements do not establish
Any multi-replica cost (RF 1). Any disk-bound behaviour (memtable-resident, no compaction crossed). The write-to-searchable interval of the vector index: the freshness probe exercised a storage-attached index on a scalar field, found the document on the first query in forty of forty idle cycles, and measured an end-to-end interval dominated by two HTTP round trips (idle median 19–25 ms, p99 54–78 ms; under 50 writes/s median 42 ms, p99 54–59 ms). On the MongoDB side, only the single-field-mutation axis is measured (appendix, measurement M13, same host, matched durability and resources); read, search, aggregation and index freshness on MongoDB remain unmeasured. Anything about another version of HCD or the Data API, or about Apache Cassandra 5.0 or 6.0.

Limitations of this article

Stated here rather than buried, because an article that grades its claims owes the reader an account of where the grading came out badly.

Four substantive claims could not be verified against sources of acceptable rank within the research budget, and all four concern the decomposing engine rather than its comparator: the enumeration of generic column names (section 2.1); the read-modify-write cycle underlying steps one to three (section 3); the consistency level at which Data API writes execute; and the current general-availability status of MongoDB search per edition. The first three were subsequently measured on one build — HCD 2.0.6 with Data API v1.0.33, one node, RF = 1 — and hold there (appendix, measurements M1–M4); they carry the M marker, which is weaker than D: an observation of a build is not a vendor's commitment. The fourth remains unverified. None is asserted here as documented.

That the unverified residue now falls almost entirely on one side is itself a finding worth stating, and it cuts against the side I work for. MongoDB's mutation semantics, its index policy and its asynchronous search path are all documented in version-pinned reference material and were confirmed verbatim. The equivalent statements about the Data API's physical representation are not published anywhere I could reach: they circulate as engineering description and practitioner lore — and, since this revision, as a measurement on one build, which is a different thing from documentation. A reader may reasonably conclude that the system I know better is the one whose mechanism is harder to verify — and may reasonably weight the comparison accordingly. One asymmetry that earlier editions carried has since been closed on the axis this article is about: MongoDB's single-field mutation was measured against HCD's on the same host, in a matched regime, with durability and resources matched (appendix, measurement M13). It confirmed the structural prediction of section 3 rather than overturning it — MongoDB's in-place update is far cheaper on this axis, by a wide margin, even when a wildcard index is added to match HCD's automatic indexing. The eleven-to-zero measurement asymmetry this section once conceded no longer holds for the mutation axis; it still holds for read, search and index freshness, where nothing on the MongoDB side was measured.

Three product pages could not be rendered during verification — the HCD 2.0 Data API limits page and the HCD 2.0 data-model page principally — with the consequence that several product figures rest on the equivalent commercial documentation for the same API surface rather than on the exact edition under discussion. These substitutions are marked in the appendix entry concerned.

Everything in section 8 concerning Cassandra 6.0 describes software in alpha, explicitly designated by its publishers as unsuitable for anything beyond development and testing. Its configuration surface, its syntax and its feature scope may all change before general availability. Treat that section as a statement about a direction of travel, never as a specification.

Three sources were consulted through list mirrors rather than the Apache archive directly, and two through the project's pull-request traffic rather than published documentation. These are marked individually. Where the distinction bears on the weight of a claim, the claim carries the R marker rather than D.
