Abstract — Part I of two

Two document databases expose near-identical interfaces — JSON in, JSON out — and diverge sharply in what a single-field update costs. This part argues that the divergence is derivable rather than incidental: it follows from one decision about how a document is laid out on disk, and that decision fixes the price of every mutation thereafter.

A document handed to the decomposing engine is shredded into one row of generic derived columns, flattened to dotted paths at any depth of nesting. Changing one field is therefore not a targeted write but a sequence of five operations, of which a query trace shows the first three directly. Index maintenance on that path covers every indexed field the document carries, not the fields that changed — which an earlier revision of this work stated the other way round, and measurement corrected.

Holding the mutation constant while the document’s untouched indexed content grew raised median latency 7.50×, against a read control of 1.56× and a constant network payload. Later campaigns resolved how that price decomposes: the driver is indexed bytes rather than field count, and a per-field rate reported in an earlier revision has been withdrawn.

A boundary condition arrived with the answer. Every rate here was measured with the cycle’s internal read served from memory; forcing it onto the SSTable path collapses the growth with indexed volume behind a large fixed read cost. The quantities in this part are memtable-regime quantities and are labelled as such wherever they appear. The comparison earlier revisions could not make has also been made: on one host, in one session, under a declared durability mapping, the decomposing engine's cost per mutation runs from roughly three to roughly thirteen times a capability-matched MongoDB's across this series, the gap widening with indexed volume. A ratio of slopes puts it at forty, on a denominator too near zero to bear that weight; the range is what the measurement supports. That is one axis, and it is the axis where decomposition is expected to lose. Two more went the same way for a different reason: through the interface it exposes, the decomposing engine neither aggregates server-side nor counts past a documented threshold, so on the analytical axis the gap is one of capability rather than speed.

Part II takes up the other half of the same decision: when what you wrote becomes visible to a search, why that is a correctness property rather than a performance one, and what a change of consensus protocol does and does not touch.

A note on epistemic status. Comparative writing about competing databases fails most often not through malice but through the silent promotion of plausible mechanism to documented fact. Every substantive claim below therefore carries one of four markers, and the appendix records the exact passage on which each rests.

D Documented. Established by the vendor's own version-pinned documentation, by the project's source of record, or by peer-reviewed publication. Quoted verbatim in the appendix.

R Reported. Described publicly by the engineers who built the system, or by the upstream project, but not restated in the versioned documentation of the shipped product. Directionally reliable; not a contractual guarantee.

U Unverified. Asserted here on structural grounds, or widely repeated in the practitioner literature, but not established by any source of acceptable rank that I was able to consult. Treat as a hypothesis to be tested against your own build.

M Measured. Observed on a named build under stated conditions, by a harness reproduced in the appendix. Stronger than U, weaker than D: it establishes what one build did on one day, not what the vendor commits to. Every M claim names its measurement (M1–M4) and every measurement names its conditions.

1. The phenomenon

A pattern recurs in design reviews with sufficient regularity to warrant formal treatment.

A team ships a service backed by a document store, and for a while it behaves. Over some months the schema accretes: a status enumeration, two or three timestamps, a nested object carrying audit provenance. Read latency holds. Write latency does not, and it degrades disproportionately to the additional bytes committed. Because no one has altered the write pattern, no one examines the write path. The investigation goes to the application tier, to the network, to garbage collection, and returns empty-handed.

The explanation is neither a defect, nor a tuning deficiency, nor a regression introduced by a release. What it is, is the deterministic consequence of a decision taken long before the team arrived: how the engine physically represents a document on disk. Recover that decision, and much of the rest of the system turns predictable, including behaviours with no apparent connection to writes at all, such as the latency with which a newly written document becomes retrievable.

The argument is developed through two systems that resolve the same design question in opposite directions: the JSON document interface layered over a Cassandra-derived engine (the IBM DataStax HCD 2.0 Data API5) and MongoDB 8.x.

2. Two resolutions of the decomposition problem

2.1 Decomposition: one document, one row, many derived columns

The intuitive model holds that a document database stores documents. Some do. This one does something considerably more interesting, and the difference is the hinge of everything that follows.

R A JSON document submitted to the Data API is shredded: decomposed into a single database row bearing a generic column layout that captures, separately, the original document text, the set of paths present within it, typed value maps for each JSON scalar type, array membership and array cardinality.11 Indexes are then constructed over the filterable columns.

M The specific column names commonly cited in the practitioner literature (doc_json, exist_keys, array_contains, array_size, and the several query_*_values maps) are widely repeated but do not appear, so far as I could establish, in any version-pinned DataStax documentation or in a readable source file of the upstream project. I reproduce them below because they are useful as a mental model and because naming them makes the claim falsifiable. They should not be treated as an interface. On HCD 2.0.6 with Data API v1.0.33 (appendix, measurement M1) all nine appear under exactly these names, alongside three that the practitioner literature omits: key, the typed partition key; tx_id, a timeuuid that guards every conditional write; and query_lexical_value, the lexical column. Nine storage-attached indexes were created on that collection, which declared no vector option. That is one build's answer, not an interface; verify against the build you deploy.

M Nesting is where this decomposition earns its keep and shows its edges, and the flat documents of the probes above never touched it. A nested document is flattened to dotted paths: every scalar, at any depth, lands in the typed value map for its type, keyed by its full path: payer.account.id, meta.flags.risk, lines.0.qty. A nested field therefore becomes filterable with no index declaration, exactly as a top-level one does.41 exist_keys enumerates every path including array indices; scalar arrays carry membership tokens, which is what makes {tags: "instant"} match; array_size holds per-path lengths; and doc_json keeps the original verbatim.

Two edges are worth knowing before modelling against them. First, inside an array of objects only positional paths exist: {"lines.0.sku": "A"} filters; {"lines.sku": "A"}, meaning "any line with that sku", does not, no path aggregating the field across elements. Second, the shredder imposes hard shape limits: nesting depth at most sixteen, at most one thousand properties per indexable object, and no indexed string beyond eight thousand bytes. All three surface as SHRED_DOC_LIMIT_VIOLATION at write time, not at query time, which is the merciful failure mode but still a design constraint rather than a tuning one.

The consequence for what follows is direct. Because a mutation rewrites doc_json in full together with every derived map, the write cost of section 4 grows with the breadth and depth of the nesting, not only with the count of top-level fields. A deeply nested document is a wide document by another name.

D Easily underestimated, and real: any field becomes filterable without anyone declaring an index for it. There is no migration to add a predicate, no ticket to a database administrator to render a new attribute searchable. The upstream project's configuration record fixes the arithmetic: ten indexes are required per collection, against fifty available per database by default1 — five collections before exhaustion, on the upstream defaults. (That figure is the budget the API checks at creation; the collection measured in the appendix, created without a vector option, received nine — measurement M1.) The shipped HCD product raises the ceiling through a guardrail set to one hundred total storage-attached indexes.3 For a team iterating rapidly on an evolving schema, this is a genuine accelerant.

M It is also the one claim in this diptych that a later comparison confirmed in the platform’s favour, so it is worth stating with its number. Against a million documents, filtering on a field nobody had declared an index for: MongoDB as ordinarily deployed scans the collection and answers in 377 ms; the decomposing engine, having indexed that field without being asked, answers in 17.4 ms.48 Twenty-two times faster, and the gap is structural rather than tuned: one engine is reading an index that exists, the other is reading everything.

U That median deserves a word, because a scan is not a stable latency. Its cost depends on where in the collection the matching document happens to sit, so the distribution is wide by construction — a median of 377 ms here, against a minimum of 13 ms and a ninety-fifth percentile of 804, with a standard deviation of 258. The figure is an expected value over document placement rather than a measurement of speed, and the honest statement is that scanning a million documents costs somewhere between a few milliseconds and most of a second depending on luck, while the index answers in seventeen regardless. That is the difference worth designing around; twenty-two is a convenient summary of it and not much more.

U The repository’s re-analysis puts it more sharply, and against the platform. Because the scan’s fastest observation of fifty, 13.3 ms, beats the decomposing engine’s median of 17.4 and its slowest of 28.7, the two supports overlap and the direction of this comparison is not established: the interval of ratios runs from 0.46 to 65.8 and contains one.52 What the data show is not that one is faster than the other but that one is predictable — about seventeen milliseconds whatever the document — against a comparator whose answer takes anything from thirteen milliseconds to most of a second depending on where the document sits. Predictability is a real property and a worse headline, and it is the one this row supports.

U The same measurement bounds the claim in the same breath. Declare a wildcard index on the MongoDB side and it answers the same query in 0.73 ms, which is twenty-four times faster than the decomposing engine. The advantage is therefore real against the default and absent against the capability-matched configuration: what is being compared is a default against a decision, not a ceiling against a ceiling. A team that knows this field will be queried can have both the index and the speed on either platform. A team that does not know yet gets the index for free on one of them, and that is the whole of the accelerant.

Everything that follows is the invoice for it.

2.2 In-place mutation

D MongoDB resolves the question in the opposite direction, and its documentation is explicit on all three components of that resolution.

Atomicity. "In MongoDB, write operations are atomic on the single-document level, even if modifying multiple values."34 The guarantee is bounded at the document, and the documentation is equally explicit that it stops there: when a single operation modifies several documents, each modification is atomic but the operation as a whole is not.34 This is a narrower guarantee than the phrase "atomic update" suggests to a reader arriving from a relational background, and the narrowness matters.

Absence of full rewrite. The $set reference states the advantage in comparative terms against full document replacement: "Targeted Updates: $set modifies only the specified fields, ensuring efficient updates by avoiding unnecessary writes and overhead when you work with large documents."35 The same page notes the replication corollary, that $set writes only the updated fields to the oplog. The contrast with decomposition is therefore not an inference drawn by this article; it is the distinction the vendor itself draws, between its operator and its own replacement path.

Declared indexes only. MongoDB creates exactly one index without being asked — a unique index on _id, at collection creation, which cannot be dropped.36 Every other index is created explicitly. The write therefore maintains what the schema designer decided it should maintain, and nothing else.

D One qualification is owed here, and omitting it would make the comparison less honest than it should be. MongoDB does provide an opt-in analogue of automatic indexing: the wildcard index, declared over the pattern $**, which "can support queries on any arbitrary field within documents in the collection."37 What separates the two platforms on this axis is consequently not capability but default. One indexes broadly unless instructed otherwise; the other indexes nothing unless instructed. Since defaults govern the systems nobody revisited, the difference is a meaningful one, and it is also smaller and more interesting than "automatic versus manual", which is how the comparison is usually and lazily framed.

4. The cost function of a single-field mutation

Because the document has been taken apart, changing one field cannot be a targeted write. It becomes a sequence:

  1. Read the entire document into memory.
  2. Modify it there.
  3. Rewrite the whole document, together with every derived column.
  4. Maintain the indexes — on the build measured here, over every indexed field of the document, not only the fields that changed (appendix, measurement M3).
  5. Guard the write-back with a consensus-based conditional operation, so that a concurrent writer cannot silently overwrite it.

M Steps one through three describe a read-modify-write cycle. This is the structural inference on which the article's title rests, and I must be candid about its evidentiary basis: it follows prima facie from decomposition — a derived column cannot be recomputed without the document that derives it — but I could not find it asserted in any DataStax documentation for the current Data API. The one official statement on the subject that I could locate concerns the earlier, architecturally distinct Documents API, and it says the opposite, that the engineers resolved to avoid read-before-write at all costs.12 Pace that 2020 statement, which concerns a different API generation and cannot settle the question either way. What settles it, for a build you control, is a query trace rather than a latency curve. On HCD 2.0.6 with Data API v1.0.33, with tracing enabled on the node, every updateOne carrying a single $set produced two CQL statements: a SELECT of key, tx_id and doc_json by key, then one UPDATE carrying eleven assignments — the nine derived columns, a fresh tx_id and the whole doc_json — guarded by IF tx_id = ? (appendix, measurement M2). The cycle is present on that build. A cost-versus-size probe on the same build grew in step with a same-size read and could not, on its own, separate a read from a read-plus-rewrite; it is the trace, not the curve, that carries the claim.

D Step five is on firmer ground. The upstream configuration record exposes a client-side retry count for lightweight-transaction failure, defaulting to three.1 The Paxos machinery underlying conditional writes in this engine family is documented explicitly: a prepare phase in which a proposer circulates a proposal number to the replicas of a key, promises returned by a quorum of acceptors, then a proposal of the value, and so forth.13 The consistency levels at which this executes in the Data API are M not published by any source I consulted; measured on HCD 2.0.6 with Data API v1.0.33, every statement on the collection ran at LOCAL_QUORUM and every conditional statement carried LOCAL_SERIAL as its serial level (appendix, measurement M4). Earlier drafts asserted LOCAL_SERIAL from memory; it is restored here on the strength of a trace.

M A single node at RF = 1 satisfies both levels with one replica, so that first measurement established the names and hid the price entirely. A later run on a six-node ring with three replicas recovered it. Every conditional write executes a full three-replica Paxos round: the coordinator reads the precondition, sends a prepare to the other two, both promise the ballot and return the read row, the coordinator proposes, both accept and append to commitlog and Paxos memtable, then commit. Coordinator-side median: 13.7 ms for the conditional UPDATE against 6.6 ms for a non-conditional SELECT (appendix, measurement M5).39 Roughly double, and that factor is the fifth operation's true cost — invisible on one node, unavoidable on a real deployment, and untouched by any decision about indexing.

The net effect — two CQL statements for an update and for a delete, one for an insert, on the build measured (M2) — is a write materially heavier than a blind CQL insertion, and — importantly — one that is not idempotent in the way a blind insertion is. Neither this nor MongoDB's single-step alternative is a defect. Both are the determinate consequence of a storage decision.

4.1 The coefficient the vendor does not publish

M Of the five steps, the fourth is the one this article stated wrongly in every prior draft, and the correction is the most consequential finding in it. The claim was that a mutation maintains "the indexes covering the fields that changed". On the build measured, it does not. It maintains the indexes covering every indexed field of the document, whether or not the mutation touched them — which follows inevitably from the trace in the preceding paragraph, since an UPDATE that rewrites all nine derived columns necessarily invalidates and rebuilds every index attached to them.

The difference between those two statements is not rhetorical, and it was measured. Holding the mutation constant at one scalar $set, and growing only the indexed content the document carries — from one such field to seventeen, each of them an eight-thousand-byte string the mutation never touches — median latency rose from 12.1 ms to 90.8 ms, a factor of 7.50. Over the same range a same-size read by key rose 1.56×, and the HTTP request payload was constant at 122–124 bytes throughout. Neither the network nor the read path accounts for the gap.38

M A multiplier is a poor form for this result, and an earlier revision of this article compounded the problem by converting it into the wrong rate. Running the same document sizes twice — once with the added content left unindexed, once with the same bytes carried as indexed fields — and subtracting at equal byte counts isolates the indexing term from the storage term:

The stronger reading is the correct one: the absolute rise across the series falls from 100.8 ms to 55.6 ms, so the per-byte term itself is halved on the SSTable path rather than merely dwarfed by a constant. The per-kilobyte rate is therefore a memtable phenomenon in both senses. Where the read leaves memory, it is the read path that decides the cost of a mutation, not the quantity of indexed content. The mechanism of section 4.1 is unaffected: the cycle is still a read, a rewrite of everything, and an index maintenance pass. What does not transpose is the number.

U Two reservations bound that in turn, and they run in opposite directions. Flushing before every mutation produces one SSTable per flush, so the internal read merges an unnaturally fragmented set — up to thirty-odd small tables — which a normal compaction schedule would not leave lying about. The fixed cost above is therefore an upper bound, and a realistic disk-bound figure sits somewhere between the memtable runs and this worst case. In the other direction, the operating system page cache could not be dropped for want of root, so "SSTable path" still does not mean "cold platter". A third reservation is one of provenance rather than method: the post-flush record does not state its topology or replication factor, while the memtable figures it is set against come from a three-replica ring. Until that is reconciled, the contrast between ×5.94 and ×1.52 may mix the read path with a change of deployment — the same confound this article objects to elsewhere, and it would be dishonest to object to it there and not here. The honest statement is that the rate is bounded below by a memtable regime and above by a fragmented-read one, and that no measurement in this study occupies the middle.

M Two passes, thirty timed repetitions per configuration after five discarded warm-ups, on a three-replica ring in a proven disk-bound regime.43 The design carried a free internal control: one configuration — sixteen fields of 4 096 bytes — belongs to both series and was therefore measured twice per pass. The two readings agreed to 2.0 % and 0.7 %, which is what licenses the slopes above; had they disagreed by more than ten per cent, nothing in this subsection would be reportable.

U The strict verdict rule, declared before the run, asked for a fields-series endpoint ratio at or below 1.15 to call bytes dominant. The two passes came in at 1.128 and 1.187 — they straddle the threshold, so the pre-registered verdict is formally inconclusive. I report that rather than quietly adopting the favourable pass. Neither passes approaches the 1.5 that would make field count an independent driver, and the direction is not in doubt; the honest statement is that bytes dominate and that the exact size of the residual field term is not pinned down.

The practical consequence is a change of question. A design review should not ask how many indexed fields sit on the mutation path. It should ask how many kilobytes of indexed content do — which is a different quantity, arrived at differently, and the one you are actually charged for.

On this build, the price of a mutation is set by how wide the document is indexed, not by how much of it you changed. Automatic indexing is charged per write, per indexed field, forever — not once, at insertion.

Three consequences follow, and they are design consequences rather than tuning ones.

Adding a field is a write-latency decision. The opening phenomenon of this article — latency creeping upward while the write pattern never changed — now carries a coefficient. A team that widens its schema is buying write cost on every mutation of every document of that shape, including mutations that never go near the new field.

Collection density is bounded by arithmetic, not by taste. M A collection created with default options and no vector dimension received nine storage-attached indexes,38 against platform guardrails of ten per table and one hundred in total — the latter a ceiling per node, across every keyspace, not per database.3 That gives roughly eleven such collections per node before the guardrail refuses — and a collection declaring a vector option consumes a tenth index, arriving exactly at the per-table threshold.

Selective indexing stops being an optimisation and becomes, for some payloads, compulsory. M The Data API rejects any indexed string longer than 8 000 bytes.38 A document carrying a description, a transcript or a chunk of source text cannot be stored at all unless that field is excluded from indexing at collection creation. The mechanism that reduces the coefficient is therefore already in your hands, and for large text you are obliged to reach for it.

Which is the practical upshot, and it is worth stating without hedging: decide what is indexed, per collection, at creation. The clause costs one line. The default costs 7.50× on the mutation path of a wide document, and costs it silently.

4.3 Where that cost is actually paid

M A rate tells you what a mutation costs. It does not tell you which component spends it, and the two are different design objects. The work described above is done in two places: the Data API tier parses the JSON, shreds it into eleven derived values and builds the statement pair, all of which scales with indexed bytes; the storage engine then executes the pair and maintains the indexes, which also scales with indexed bytes. Nothing in the rate distinguishes them.

Separating them takes a subtraction. Run the same mutation through the Data API, then run the identical CQL statement pair directly against the same row with the shredded values read back rather than recomputed: the storage work is the same, the tier work is absent, and the difference is the tier.45

I wrote that "nothing forced that agreement". Arithmetic forced it. M There is a real replication in the same numbers, and it is not that one. The total slope was measured at 0.763 and 0.790 ms per indexed kilobyte in one campaign, on one keyspace; it came back at 0.772 in a later campaign, on a different keyspace, on a different day, through a different instrument.45 Nothing forced that agreement, and it is the closest thing to an independent confirmation the study contains.

U A second, independent estimate of the tier — subtracting coordinator-side durations from client-observed latency, using query traces rather than a bypass — puts it lower, at 0.145 ms per KiB and nineteen per cent. The two methods agree to within a quarter at 8, 16 and 32 KiB and diverge beyond it, by 34 % at 64 KiB and 28 % at 125 KiB. They subtract different things: the bypass removes a complete client-side CQL execution, the trace removes only the coordinator's internal duration. A second dispersion compounds the first, and it sits inside the bypass method rather than between methods: its two passes returned tier slopes of 0.190 and 0.248, thirty per cent apart, which is wider than the quarter this study treats as disqualifying between instruments. The coefficients of determination above describe the fit within a pass, not the agreement between them. No single precise split is therefore published here. What survives both methods is the direction and the order of magnitude: the tier is a substantial minority, the storage engine the majority.

Which matters for a reason that has nothing to do with milliseconds. Between a fifth and a third of this cost sits in a component that scales out by adding instances. The rest sits in one that does not. The criticism this article makes of automatic indexing is therefore softened in part — and only in part, the centre of gravity staying where it was.

U A later measurement destabilised the split further, and in the direction that costs the platform. Adding a third arm to the comparative probe — HCD driven straight through CQL, the tier bypassed entirely, on the same host and the same series — put the slope at 0.704 ms per KiB against 0.777 through the Data API. Removing the tier removed nine per cent of the rate, not the twenty-nine per cent the subtraction above reports.47 The two estimates differ threefold and were taken under different regimes and interleavings, and neither rests on separated supports: at every size, the Data API and direct-CQL ranges intersect. What the pair establishes is that the tier is a minority; where in the range from a tenth to a third it falls is not settled, and this article should not be read as settling it. The practical consequence points one way: on the later, cleaner measurement, roughly nine tenths of the cost sits in the component that does not scale out.

U A further dispersion sits in the subtrahend itself. The direct-CQL arm is markedly noisier than the arm it is subtracted from: at 8 KiB its median is 17.9 ms against a ninety-fifth percentile of 61.9, and at 64 KiB its minimum is 14.3 against a median of 49.3. A distribution that wide is not plausibly unimodal, and subtracting its median from a cleaner one yields a tier term whose uncertainty this study does not quantify. The direction survives that; a decimal place does not.

4.6 Two habits that follow directly

The difference surfaces in two places under your control: how you write, and what identifier you assign the record.

# astrapy — the Python client for the Data API
#
# (1) Server-generated identifier: convenient, and NOT safe to retry.
# If the network fails after the write applied, a retry creates a second document.
collection.insert_one({"payer": "acct-42", "amount_cents": 125000})

# (2) Identifier derived from the business key: a retry is rejected as a duplicate,
# so the operation becomes naturally idempotent. Same cost, far better failure mode.
collection.insert_one({
    "_id": "pmt-2026-08-07-000123",   # from the payment reference, not random
    "payer": "acct-42",
    "amount_cents": 125000,
})

That single choice eliminates an entire category of production incident, on either platform, at no runtime cost. It is the cheapest correctness improvement available in this class of system.

The second habit is to let the engine apply the change rather than performing it yourself:

# Anti-pattern: read-modify-write in application code.
# Three round trips, and two concurrent workers can silently lose one update.
doc = collection.find_one({"_id": "pmt-...000123"})
doc["status"] = "SETTLED"
collection.replace_one({"_id": doc["_id"]}, doc)   # rewrites everything you read

# Better: transmit the intent, not the document.
# One round trip. Whatever the engine does internally, it does it once,
# atomically, and without shipping the document across the wire twice.
collection.update_one(
    {"_id": "pmt-2026-08-07-000123"},
    {"$set": {"status": "SETTLED"}},
)

The opening phenomenon now resolves itself. Write latency crept upward because the team added fields, and each added field was silently enrolled in the maintained index set. Nothing about their write pattern changed. The cost of their write pattern did.

5. What decomposition costs, and the design rule that follows

The cost localises in one identifiable regime: workloads that repeatedly mutate the same record. Counters. Status machines transiting a sequence of states. Hot aggregates updated on every event. There, the in-place update is structurally cheaper, and no quantity of tuning alters the structure.

Model for append and read at scale, not for repeated mutation of the same record.

If your access pattern genuinely concentrates writes on individual records — and some legitimately do — that is valuable information about which engine suits that component. It is far cheaper to learn in week one than in month six, and cheaper still to learn from a storage diagram than from an incident review.

6.2 Choose what gets indexed, deliberately

Automatic indexing is a default, not a law. If a field will demonstrably never be filtered upon, excluding it renders every write to that document cheaper. On the other platform the same discipline applies mutatis mutandis, inverted in its direction: declare the indexes you need, and no more. In both cases the discipline is the same act — deciding, rather than inheriting.

6.3 Measure write-to-searchable before designing around it

It is twelve lines, and virtually nobody runs it:

import time, uuid

marker = f"probe-{uuid.uuid4()}"        # unique, so nothing else can match it
t0 = time.perf_counter()
collection.insert_one({"_id": marker, "body": marker})

while True:                              # poll until the write is findable
    if collection.find_one({"body": marker}):
        print(f"write-to-searchable: {(time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000:.0f} ms")
        break
    time.sleep(0.01)

Run it idle, then run it again under write load. Two things this probe is not. It does not tell you whether an update reads before it writes — for that, enable query tracing on one node for a handful of updates and read system_traces, the method behind measurement M2. And, on evidence, it does not cleanly measure index lag either: what it returns is an interval that contains any index lag alongside two network round trips, and on the build measured here the lag term was zero and the transport term was all of it. Record the attempt count, not just the elapsed time — a document found on the first query has no measurable lag, whatever the stopwatch says, and a rising attempt count is the only unambiguous signal that an index is behind. The second run is the one that matters, because it is under load that the two architectures diverge and under load that your users will encounter whichever behaviour you inherited. A quarter of an hour establishes whether index freshness is a constraint you must design around or one you may safely ignore. If you are building anything with retrieval in it, this is the highest-yield fifteen minutes in this article.

7. What to do this week

Both apply whatever database you run today, and neither takes longer than an afternoon.

1 — Inventory the indexed content on your mutation path. Thirty minutes.
For each entity your service updates frequently, write down how many kilobytes of it are indexed — the field count matters, but far less than the volume inside those fields, and nested paths count too. That quantity, not the size of your change, is what you pay on every write. On the build measured, roughly 0.77 ms per indexed kilobyte per mutation; your own number will differ and is worth having. Where it is large and the mutable subset is small, the remedy is one clause at collection creation on the decomposing engine, or one fewer createIndex on the other.

2 — Establish who generates your record identifiers. One hour.
Walk the writes that matter — payments, orders, anything on which a duplicate would be expensive — and determine whether the identifier derives from your business data or from the database. Convert the ones that matter. This eliminates a category of production incident, costs nothing at runtime, and behaves identically on every database in this class.

3 — Ask your own database how it stores a record. An afternoon.
Whatever you operate, establish whether it keeps a record whole or takes it apart, and what it indexes automatically. Then re-read your slowest write path in that light. In the majority of cases the explanation is already there, and the investigation has been examining the wrong layer.

8. Conclusion to Part I

What this part establishes. A document handed to the decomposing engine is not stored as a document. It is shredded into one row of twelve columns — nine derived value maps, the document text, a partition key and a transaction identifier — over which nine storage-attached indexes are created without anyone asking. Changing one scalar field is therefore not a targeted write: a query trace shows a SELECT of the whole row followed by an UPDATE carrying eleven assignments, guarded by a conditional on the transaction identifier. Nesting is flattened to dotted paths, so depth and breadth both enter the cost. These are direct readings of the engine’s own schema and trace, reproduced across passes; they are the strongest evidence in this diptych, and the vendor publishes none of them.

What follows, with its coefficient. Index maintenance on a mutation covers every indexed field the document carries, not the fields that changed — an earlier revision of this article stated the reverse, and measurement corrected it. The cost rises with indexed content the mutation never touches, and the driver is indexed bytes rather than field count: at constant volume, multiplying the field count fourteenfold moved the median by thirteen to nineteen per cent; at constant field count, multiplying the bytes sixteenfold moved it by a factor of four and a half along a line of near-perfect fit. The rate measured about 0.77 ms per indexed kilobyte.

And the boundary of that coefficient, which matters as much as the coefficient. Every rate here was taken with the cycle’s internal read served from memory. Forcing that read onto the SSTable path halves the per-byte term and introduces a fixed cost of roughly a hundred milliseconds that dominates it. The rate is a memtable-regime quantity. The mechanism — read, rewrite everything, maintain every index — is regime-independent; the number is not, and no figure in this part should be carried into a disk-bound design without being re-measured there.

Against the comparator, on this axis, the decomposing engine loses, and the loss is structural. On one host, in one session, under a durability mapping that favoured it, and with the stateless tier subtracted from both sides of the argument, mutating one field costs between three and thirteen times more, the gap widening with indexed volume. Fetching one document by identifier costs about twenty times more. Only about a tenth of the mutation disadvantage lies in the stateless tier; the rest is the engine. This is what the layout decision predicts, and it is the price of what the layout buys.

What it buys, measured. On a million documents, filtering on a field for which nobody declared an index costs 377 ms against a comparator that must scan, and 17 ms here. That is a real operational advantage, and it is an advantage against a default rather than against a ceiling: the same comparator, given a wildcard index, answers in 0.73 ms. What the platform offers is not speed but the absence of a design decision — you never pay for the index you forgot to declare.

What this part does not establish. A disk-bound read without the artificial fragmentation the measurement introduced. Concurrency, multiple clients, multiple hosts. Aggregation. The internal decomposition of the tier’s own cost. Any confidence interval: thirty repetitions and two passes give shapes, not precisions.

Everything was measured on one host, one build of each engine, with almost no regime outside the cache. Nothing was measured under concurrency, and production is concurrent. And the profiles named in the first column are descriptions rather than measurements: no real application’s write-to-search interval was timed, so a reader recognising their own system in a row is making a judgement the study cannot make for them.

Establish how your database physically stores a record and you can predict most of what it will cost you. That claim survives seven campaigns, including the five axes it predicts unfavourably for the platform this author works on. What it does not do is favour an engine.

Limitations

Stated here rather than buried, because work that grades its claims owes the reader an account of where the grading came out badly. These limitations apply to the diptych as a whole.

Four substantive claims could not be verified against sources of acceptable rank, and three of the four concern the decomposing engine rather than its comparator: the enumeration of generic column names, the read-modify-write cycle, the consistency levels at which Data API writes execute, and the current general-availability status of MongoDB search per edition. The first three were subsequently measured and hold on the builds named; they carry the M marker, which is weaker than D: an observation of a build is not a vendor’s commitment. The fourth has since been resolved from the vendor’s own pages; Part II carries the correction.

That the unverified residue falls almost entirely on one side is itself a finding, and it cuts against the side I work for. MongoDB’s mutation semantics, its index policy and its asynchronous search path are documented in version-pinned reference material and were confirmed verbatim. The equivalent statements about the Data API’s physical representation are not published anywhere I could reach. A reader may reasonably conclude that the system I know better is the one whose mechanism is harder to verify, and may weight the comparison accordingly. An earlier revision added that the asymmetry ran further still, nine measurements having been taken on one side and none on the other. That is no longer the case: section 4.4 reports the identical series run against both engines on one host. The documentary asymmetry above remains; the measurement asymmetry does not.

The four ratios most likely to be quoted are the least supported. The 44×, 79×, 40× and 72× are quotients of fitted slopes. The distribution-free apparatus that certifies the rest of this work compares pairs of samples and fits nothing, so it reaches none of them. Fitted properly, with the standard errors carried through, the denominator is not distinguishable from zero on the second of the two passes and the ratio is unbounded there; the point estimate moves by a factor of 2.6 between passes. Section 4.4 states this. A reader who takes one number from this diptych should take a latency range, never a slope ratio.

No confidence interval can be computed from this study by anyone, including its author. Every probe but one discarded the per-observation series and kept only the summary — count, minimum, median, ninety-fifth and ninety-ninth percentiles, maximum, standard deviation. What survives permits exactly one distribution-free question: whether the two arms’ ranges are disjoint. Where they are, the direction is certain and an exact permutation probability follows; where they are not, the direction is not established from these data, whatever the medians suggest. Section 4.4 carries the answer for every axis. Two of the three margins that favour the decomposing engine do not survive it as latency comparisons; one of them survives as a proportion.

The shape of the cost is now resolved in one direction and not the other. The byte term is established across two passes with an internal control agreeing to under two per cent; the residual field term is not pinned down, the two passes straddling the pre-registered threshold at 1.128 and 1.187. The separation of the Data API tier’s parsing cost from the storage engine’s has since been done and is reported in section 4.3, with the qualification that its two methods diverge at the larger sizes and only a range is defensible. The per-kilobyte rate was also measured only in the disk regime; the near-perfect linearity is expected to survive a memtable regime but was not re-run there.

The disk-bound caveat has been discharged, and replaced by a narrower one. An earlier revision noted that every regime was memtable-resident and that the engine described never touched its disk. A three-replica run in a proven disk-bound state — 6.05 GiB on disk against a 2 048 MiB memtable budget, forty SSTables, seventy-eight compactions crossed, caches invalidated — has since been executed.44 The indexed-content coefficient did not collapse there: ×5.94 against ×7.50 in the original memtable run.

M That sentence, as an earlier revision left it, claimed more than the run supported, and the correction matters enough to state rather than quietly absorb. The dataset was on disk; the row the probe read was not. A read-modify-write cycle re-reads what it has just written, and that row sits in the memtable whatever surrounds it. The campaign proved a disk-resident dataset, never a disk-resident read. When the read is actually forced onto the SSTable path, the coefficient does collapse, from ×5.94 to ×1.52, and section 4.1 reports what replaces it. What that campaign established is narrower than its label: memory pressure and cold caches do not destroy the coefficient. Leaving memory does.

What remains is a page-cache confound and a hot-row confound, and they matter. The operating system page cache was not dropped, root on the host being unavailable, and the 6 GiB dataset fits comfortably in the host's memory: "disk-bound" here means the data genuinely exceeds the memtable budget and lives in SSTables, not that any given read missed RAM. Further, the read-modify-write probe re-reads the row it has just written, and that row is memtable-resident whatever the size of the dataset around it. The disk regime therefore acts through memory pressure and cold caches rather than by serving the probed row from disk. Anyone reading these figures as fully disk-resident latencies is reading more into them than was measured.

Two product pages could not be rendered during verification — the HCD 2.0 Data API limits page and the HCD 2.0 data-model page — so several product figures rest on the equivalent commercial documentation for the same API surface rather than on the exact edition under discussion. These substitutions are marked in the appendix entry concerned.

Everything concerning Cassandra 6.0 describes software in alpha, explicitly designated by its publishers as unsuitable for anything beyond development and testing. Its configuration surface, its syntax and its feature scope may all change before general availability.

Two sources were consulted through list mirrors rather than the Apache archive directly, and one through the project’s pull-request traffic rather than published documentation. Where that provenance bears on the weight of a claim, the claim carries the R marker rather than D — with one exception I would rather name than let a reader find. The alpha classification quoted in Part II is marked D although the only reachable copy of that announcement was a list mirror. The quotation is verbatim and the tag dates corroborate it from the project repository itself, but by the rule stated in this very paragraph it ought to carry R. Consistency is a property of a system of markers, not of any one of them, and this is where mine failed.
