Abstract — Part II of two

Where index maintenance sits relative to the commit decides whether a system can read back what it just wrote. One engine pays for indexing on the write path and is searchable at acknowledgement; the other maintains its search indexes outside the transaction commit window, asynchronously, and is faster to write for it. Neither is a defect: it is the same cost placed in two different places.

Two kinds of index have to be separated before that claim means anything, and a comparative campaign forced the separation. Ordinary secondary indexes are maintained synchronously by both engines, so the index is current on both and on that question they are at parity — though not on what a caller waits for, which ranges from 7 ms to 894 ms across the three configurations measured. The asymmetry is confined to the search and vector index, and there it has been measured: a document just written is returned by search on the first query on one engine, and not for two thirds of a second on average on the other — a periodic refresh spread from a tenth of a second to a second and a fifth — while remaining findable by its identifier throughout. Whether that costs anything depends on how soon the search arrives: sweeping that delay shows the asynchronous path missing every document at zero, half of them at half a second, and none at all beyond one. This is one of two axes of eight in this diptych that the decomposing engine wins, and the only one it wins on an exactly tested result — a miss-rate, the latency ranges overlapping and establishing nothing: absolutely inside the comparator’s refresh window and not at all outside — a window that measured about a second on this deployment and is a property of that deployment, not of the product. Narrower, and more conditional, than earlier revisions of this part claimed.

For a catalogue the difference is invisible. For a system that composes a write and a search without waiting, it is categorical. A system that records a fact and cannot retrieve it is producing a wrong answer, not a slow one, and the failure is invisible to latency instrumentation by construction. This part argues that the write-to-searchable interval belongs in acceptance criteria rather than in a performance backlog, and supplies a control that lets a reader put their own number to it.

It then qualifies its own side’s guarantee. The Data API’s consistency levels are configuration rather than fixed behaviour, and they are asymmetric: writes commit at quorum while vector search reads a single replica. Measured on a co-located ring the resulting window was not detectable; a wide-area deployment is where it would surface, and this bench cannot reproduce that.

The part closes on the fifth operation — the consensus round guarding a conditional write, now priced at three replicas — and on its designated successor in Apache Cassandra, which turns out to leave the four storage steps of Part I untouched.

A note on epistemic status. Comparative writing about competing databases fails most often not through malice but through the silent promotion of plausible mechanism to documented fact. Every substantive claim below therefore carries one of four markers, and the appendix records the exact passage on which each rests.

D Documented. Established by the vendor's own version-pinned documentation, by the project's source of record, or by peer-reviewed publication. Quoted verbatim in the appendix.

R Reported. Described publicly by the engineers who built the system, or by the upstream project, but not restated in the versioned documentation of the shipped product. Directionally reliable; not a contractual guarantee.

U Unverified. Asserted here on structural grounds, or widely repeated in the practitioner literature, but not established by any source of acceptable rank that I was able to consult. Treat as a hypothesis to be tested against your own build.

M Measured. Observed on a named build under stated conditions, by a harness reproduced in the appendix. Stronger than U, weaker than D: it establishes what one build did on one day, not what the vendor commits to. Every M claim names its measurement (M1–M4) and every measurement names its conditions.

1. What decomposition purchases

Three properties, of which the third is the one comparative treatments most consistently omit.

Queryability without index design. Already treated, and genuinely valuable early in a product's life, when the schema is still an argument rather than a contract.

Write scale-out without a coordinating primary. No single node must accept every write, so capacity is added by adding nodes rather than by enlarging one.

An index current at the moment the write completes. D Because indexing occurs on the write path, a document becomes searchable as soon as it is written. The HCD 2.0 documentation states this directly, and attributes it to the vector index implementation by name:

"When you insert data, JVector adds those new documents to the graph immediately, so you can efficiently search right away."

DataStax, About vector databases, Hyper-Converged Database 2.0.4

M One qualifier that sentence omits, and it only appears above a single replica. The Data API's consistency levels are configuration properties rather than fixed behaviour, and its defaults are asymmetric: writes and ordinary reads commit at LOCAL_QUORUM, but vector search reads at LOCAL_ONE.39 "Searchable as soon as it is written" is therefore a single-replica guarantee, not a quorum one: at RF > 1 a vector query issued at acknowledgement can in principle read the one replica the write has not yet reached.

M Measured on a six-node, two-datacentre ring with the collection at three replicas, the effect was not detectable: in 120 of 120 cycles the just-written vector was the top match on the first LOCAL_ONE query, and in no cycle did the quorum key read succeed while the vector search missed.40 Sub-millisecond inter-replica latency closes the gap far below the round trip that carries the query. The window is therefore real in the protocol and bounded below the measurement floor on that ring — and a wide-area deployment, where inter-replica latency is tens of milliseconds, is precisely where it would surface. Which is the same window this article attributes to the other engine, arriving here by a different route and at a different scale. The guarantee is real; its scope is one replica; and a reader deploying across datacentres should measure it there rather than inherit it from this page.

Nothing here is a measurement. On the build this article did instrument, no index lag was detectable below a floor of roughly one network round trip; for any system you have not instrumented, the honest value is unknown, which is the reason this control exists.

For a product catalogue, several seconds of index lag is invisible; no user experiences it and no requirement references it. For anything that reads back what it has just written, it is not a performance detail at all: a system that records a fact and cannot retrieve it is producing a wrong answer, not a slow one, however promptly the index subsequently converges. How soon the read arrives decides whether that matters, and section 1.2 puts a number on it. The failure is semantic, it is invisible to latency instrumentation, and it will be reported by users as hallucination rather than as staleness. A fortiori, any system that writes and reads within a single conversational turn inherits the interval as a hard correctness bound rather than as a service objective.

M Measured, that interval turns out not to be an index interval at all. Across forty idle cycles on HCD 2.0.6, the freshly written marker was found on the first attempt forty times out of forty; the attempt histogram has a single entry.38 Decomposed, the 22.9 ms median splits into an insert of 9.8 ms and the one retrieval of 11.2 ms that followed it: two HTTP round trips, and nothing else. No index lag was detectable at all, which means the harness's own threshold — it declared the probe inconclusive for exceeding 50 ms under load — was measuring transport rather than indexing. A harness that reports the wrong thing precisely is still reporting the wrong thing, and the attempt histogram is what caught it.

Two caveats bound that result; none softens it. The retrieval exercised a storage-attached index on a scalar field, not the vector graph the quotation above concerns; and one node at RF = 1 removes any replication interval by construction. The later vector probe, at three replicas, used vectors of sixteen dimensions — between one and two orders of magnitude below the embeddings a retrieval system actually stores — and the cost of maintaining a graph index synchronously at production dimensions was not measured. What is established is that on this build, on this path, the write-to-searchable interval is indistinguishable from the cost of asking.

If you are building a system of that kind, the write-to-searchable interval belongs in your acceptance criteria, not in your performance backlog.

M A distinction has to be drawn here that earlier revisions of this part did not draw, and drawing it removes most of the territory this section thought it held. There are two kinds of index in play, and only one of them is contested. Ordinary secondary indexes are maintained synchronously by both engines: a later comparison wrote a document and queried it through one, on a million-document collection, and all three arms found it on the first attempt, forty times out of forty.41 The index was current everywhere, and on that question there is parity.

M The elapsed time is not at parity in either direction, and an earlier revision of this section lost the distinction by calling the whole thing a tie. Writing a document and then retrieving it through a secondary index took 894 ms against MongoDB as ordinarily deployed, 33 ms against the decomposing engine, and 7 ms against MongoDB under a wildcard index.41 Twenty-seven times faster than the first, four and a half times slower than the second.

Which is the distinction this part exists to draw, arriving from an unexpected direction. Index currency — whether the write is in the index yet — is a tie on ordinary indexes. Time to answer is not, and for a user or an assistant waiting on a reply, the second is what the first feels like. Against the configuration a team actually deploys, the automatic index turns almost a second into a thirtieth of one. Against a configuration that declared its index, it does the opposite.

The asymmetry this part describes concerns the other kind: the search and vector index, synchronous on one engine and maintained asynchronously through change streams on the other. That is the axis where the window exists, and it is where the argument of this section lives.

M That axis has now been measured, on one host in one session, and it is the only axis in this diptych where the decomposing engine wins decisively. D One asymmetry is not a matter of deployment and should not be phrased as if it were. The window on the asynchronous side has a width, and that width is a setting — this deployment’s happens to be about a second, another’s would differ. The synchronous side has no window to widen or narrow: its index is maintained on the write path, so there is no interval, no setting that would create one, and nothing to tune. What the two deployments differ in is how much the asymmetry costs. That it exists is architectural.

U Which obliges this part to withdraw the example it opened with. M What the measurement does establish is narrower and sharper. Inside the window the asymmetry is not a tendency but a certainty: at the shortest delay the asynchronous path misses every document and the synchronous one misses none, and the failure rate is still one in two at half a second. Whether a given workload sits inside that window is the only question that matters, and it is a question about the workload. One term of it deserves naming. In a retrieval system the document is written with its vector already computed, so embedding the document sits before the write and does not enter the window; embedding the query sits inside it and consumes part of the interval — U plausibly tens to a few hundred milliseconds, an estimate this study did not measure — without closing it. A pipeline that writes the raw document and computes its vector asynchronously moves the start of the window to the vector’s arrival rather than the document’s. D The comparator now offers exactly such a pipeline as a feature: with its Automated Embedding, in preview for self-managed deployments, the search process itself generates the vector at index time rather than the application supplying it.52 A document written that way enters the search index only after both the embedding and the refresh, so the convenience widens the window this section measures rather than narrowing it — a point in the decomposing engine’s favour that the measurements here, taken with vectors supplied by the client, do not capture.

U The boundary is not a second. It is this deployment’s refresh interval, which happens to be about a second and which no user setting exposes.43 On a deployment refreshing every two hundred milliseconds the advantage would be total below two hundred milliseconds; on one refreshing every three seconds it would reach far further. The curve traces the interval, it does not fix it, and stating the finding as "below a second" would make a container default sound like a property of a product.

One engine has a window whose width is a setting. The other has none, and no setting would give it one. What your deployment decides is how much that costs you — not whether it is true.

U Four reservations, and the first two matter. The packaged image runs a single-member replica set, so a majority write concern is satisfied trivially there. That reasoning inverts itself: a quantity that depends on the deployment is precisely one this deployment cannot vouch for elsewhere. The image is, besides, the vendor’s development and testing build, whose ingestion tuning has no particular reason to match the managed service a reader would actually buy. 1.3 The opposite resolution, stated in the vendor's own terms

D MongoDB's self-managed search and vector search execute in a separate process, mongot, running alongside the database.1516 MongoDB describes the mechanism precisely:

"mongot builds and maintains these compute-intensive indexes outside of the transaction commit window by leveraging change streams to asynchronously replicate changes to source data, allowing customers to add search and vector search capabilities without fear of impacting existing transactional workloads."

MongoDB, Now Source Available: The Engine Powering MongoDB Search, 15 January 2026.14

This is a deliberate and defensible design, and it is panel B of the figure above. It removes index maintenance from the write path, which is precisely the cost the decomposing engine elects to pay. Two coherent answers to one question, with the trade legible in a single sentence from each vendor, which is a considerably better basis for a decision than any benchmark.

Note that the two designs are not in competition on a single axis. They optimise different things and the choice is properly determined by the workload, not by preference: ceteris paribus, a catalogue tolerates the window and profits from the throughput; a retrieval system does not tolerate the window at all.

D One corollary follows from the quoted mechanism and requires no further source: asynchronous replication of changes implies a non-zero interval between a write committing and the corresponding search index reflecting it. 2. Where JVector actually appears — three loci, three statuses

A persistent conflation in this area will invalidate an evaluation if carried into it. The same library appears in three distinct places with three distinct statuses, and one place where it does not appear at all.

D In Apache Cassandra 5.0 upstream: it does not. Upstream vector search through storage-attached indexing was specified and built on Lucene's Hierarchical Navigable Small World implementation. The enhancement proposal is explicit: the implementation leverages Lucene's HNSW library, characterised there as the best available ANN algorithm for Java at the time.21 Storage-attached indexing itself is a 5.0 feature.22

D In the DataStax distribution: as the shipped engine. JVector, a graph index of the DiskANN family supporting product and binary quantisation,19 is part of what the distribution adds atop the Cassandra 5.0 base,5 and the HCD documentation names it as the mechanism behind immediate searchability.4

D In OpenSearch: as an official plugin, not a core engine. The documented core k-NN engines are Lucene, Faiss and the deprecated NMSLIB, the latter superseded in favour of the first two;17 the default engine changed from NMSLIB to Faiss in the 2.18 line, and index creation on NMSLIB is blocked from OpenSearch 3.0.18 JVector is available separately as opensearch-project/opensearch-jvector, Apache-2.0 licensed and governed within the OpenSearch project, with a release train tracking OpenSearch versions.20 Its README claims thread-safe concurrent index construction, incremental merges in place of full graph rebuilds, and — directly relevant here — compatibility with Cassandra, "which allows to more easily transfer vector encoded data from Cassandra to OpenSearch and vice versa."19

The practical consequence: a benchmark of "Cassandra vector search" tells you nothing about the DataStax distribution, and a benchmark of either tells you nothing about the OpenSearch plugin. Three artefacts, three governance models, three release cadences. Establish which one your evaluation actually exercised before you read its numbers.

3. The fifth operation and its designated successor

Of the five steps above, the last is a consensus round, and it alone has a named successor already in flight. Any reader holding a current view of the Cassandra roadmap will ask about it. The answer is more interesting than a simple replacement narrative, and it is worth developing with some care, because it both qualifies and reinforces the thesis of this article.

3.1 What Accord specifies

D CEP-15 proposes Accord, a leaderless timestamp protocol for general-purpose distributed transactions. Its stated goals are transactions operating over any set of keys atomically, strict-serializable isolation, one wide-area round trip under normal conditions, failure tolerance unaffected by any minority of replica failures, absence of any introduced bottleneck, and — the goal most relevant here — support for live migration from Paxos. Two techniques are proposed as novel: a reorder buffer guaranteeing single-round-trip consensus under a timestamp protocol, and fast path electorates preserving leaderless fast-path availability under worst tolerated failure. Timestamps are hybrid logical clocks made globally unique by appending each replica's identifier. The proposal situates itself in the EPaxos lineage, claiming better properties than Caesar or Tempo.23

The single round trip is a design goal inscribed in the proposal, not a measurement. U No benchmark figures published by the Apache Software Foundation were located. Practitioners will encounter a great many performance claims about Accord in secondary material; none that I could trace to a primary source.

3.2 Coexistence, not replacement

D This is the point most frequently mangled in summary. The proposal is explicit on both horizons:

"This work is expected to replace Paxos for the project. In order to support live migration both protocols will be maintained for at least an interim period, after which it is expected that Paxos will be deprecated. The precise timing of this will be up to the community."

CEP-15, General Purpose Transactions, section "Long Term".23

And, on the architecture of the coexistence: supporting the Paxos-based lightweight transaction and Accord side by side is cited in the proposal as an instance of the intended modularity.23 No deprecation of Paxos has been announced. Lightweight transactions remain the mechanism in the field.

3.5 What Accord changes in the five-operation model — and what it does not

Here the analysis becomes unusually clean, because the proposal settles it by its own terms. Among the explicit non-goals of CEP-15 are SASI, SAI and local secondary indexes.23 Storage-attached indexing, which is step four of our five, falls outside the proposal's scope by declaration rather than by inference.

Accord alters how a write is arbitrated. It does not alter how a document is decomposed and rewritten. The thesis of this article is therefore not weakened by the arrival of a successor protocol; it is corroborated by it. A change of consensus mechanism, a genuine advance in distributed systems, formally specified and years in construction, leaves untouched the four steps that derive from physical layout. That is exactly what one should expect if the layout decision is the real predictor, and exactly what one should not expect if the five-step cost were an artefact of the consensus round. The successor protocol is, in this precise sense, a controlled experiment on the article's own hypothesis.

4. If you benchmark this yourself

None of what follows is mine, and the authors state it better than I can. The two failures that ruin most published comparisons of these systems are coordinated omission — driving load as fast as the system replies, which conceals precisely the stalls under investigation — and reporting an average where a distribution is required. Gil Tene named the first and wrote wrk2 and HdrHistogram to correct it.30 The remaining pitfalls are, inter alia, the documented weaknesses of the Yahoo! Cloud Serving Benchmark on which most published NoSQL comparisons rest.31

Four conditions decide whether anyone can interpret your numbers, and all four are disclosure rather than technique: hold durability equivalent between the systems and record the mapping; state the working set against available memory and run both an in-cache and a disk-bound regime; declare the topology, including any coordinating tier, since a stateless API tier is a provisioning decision; and run long enough to cross the background maintenance cycles rather than merely long enough to sustain the rate. The harness accompanying this article applies all of them to itself, and declares in its output record the one it failed to meet.

U That sentence was true of one harness and is not true of the campaigns as a whole, which is worth saying here rather than leaving a reader to find it. Measured against the six requirements above, this study satisfies about half. Load was driven at a fixed offered rate for the freshness probe alone; every mutation, read and comparison measurement ran closed-loop, one operation after the reply to the last — the very failure the first requirement names. One campaign out of seven reached a disk-bound regime and crossed a compaction; the rest are cache figures. Distributions rather than averages, a declared durability mapping and a declared topology are met throughout.

A study that holds others to six rules and meets three of them has less authority than its apparatus suggests, and the right response is to say so in the section that states the rules. 5. What to do this week

Measure your write-to-searchable interval. Fifteen minutes.
Run the probe from section 1 against whatever you already operate, once idle and once under write load, and record the attempt count rather than the elapsed time — a document found on the first query has no measurable lag, whatever the stopwatch says, and a rising attempt count is the only unambiguous signal that an index is behind. You will obtain a number you almost certainly do not have, and it is the number that decides whether index lag is a constraint you must design around. If you are building anything with retrieval in it, this is the highest-value quarter-hour in either part of this diptych.

And if you deploy across datacentres, measure it there.
Every freshness figure in this part comes from replicas that share a host and answer in under a millisecond. The asymmetry between a quorum write and a single-replica vector read is real in the protocol and was bounded below the measurement floor on that ring. Tens of milliseconds between replicas is exactly the condition this bench could not reproduce, and exactly where the question is open.

6. Conclusion to Part II, and to the diptych

Two kinds of index, and only one is contested. Ordinary secondary indexes are maintained synchronously by both engines: writing a document and querying it through one returned it on the first attempt in every cycle, on both sides. There is parity there and nothing for either party to claim. The asymmetry this part describes is confined to the search index — synchronous on one engine, maintained asynchronously through change streams on the other.

The asymmetry is architectural; the magnitude is a deployment property. The asynchronous path carries a periodic refresh: sampled across every phase of its cycle, the write-to-searchable lag spreads from 89 ms to 1 170, mean 646. The synchronous path has no such interval — its index sits on the write path, and no setting would give it one. The first figure this part published, a tight 1 015 ms, was an artefact of a probe that had synchronised itself to the cycle; it overstated the lag by about 1.6 times and is withdrawn.

Whether that costs anything depends entirely on the caller. At zero delay the asynchronous path misses every document and the synchronous one misses none; at half a second, one in two; from a second onward, none at all. The transition tracks the refresh interval of the deployment, not the identity of the product.

Which obliges this part to withdraw its own opening example. Every earlier revision motivated the argument with a conversational assistant. A turn driven by a language model takes seconds; the window closes before the read arrives, and the failure never occurs. What survives is narrower and sharper: where a write and a search are composed inside the refresh window, with no human and no model interposed, the difference is between a right answer and a wrong one, at a rate approaching certainty. Which real workloads sit there is a question this study did not ask.

6.3 On what this study is worth

It corrected itself four times in public, and each correction cost it something it had claimed. Index maintenance was said to cover the fields that changed; it covers every indexed field. A per-field rate was derived and published; the experiment had grown fields and bytes together and never licensed it. The per-kilobyte rate that replaced it was presented as regime-independent; forcing the read off the memtable halved it. The freshness argument was motivated by conversational retrieval; a sweep of turn latency showed the window closes first. A fifth correction was arithmetic rather than empirical: a sum presented as a replication was an identity, true by construction. Three of the four were found by the author’s own follow-up rather than by an objection, which is the only reason they are in the text rather than in a comment thread.

The standard this study sets and does not meet. Section 4 lists six conditions for a defensible comparison; the campaigns satisfy about three. Load was driven closed-loop almost everywhere — one operation awaiting the reply to the last, which is the first thing that section tells a reader not to do. One campaign of seven left the cache. Distributions rather than averages, a declared durability mapping and a declared topology are met throughout. A study that holds others to six rules and meets three has less authority than its apparatus suggests, and saying so is accuracy rather than modesty.

What none of it transposes to. One host, a shared virtual machine whose storage class could not be established. One build of each engine. No concurrency, no multiple clients, no wide-area topology, no confidence interval anywhere. Five of the instruments were written by the person they were meant to constrain, and the verdicts resting on unmodified supplied code are the sounder ones. The comparator was exercised across two deployments, and the search paths compared were not the same object.

The author is employed by a company that sells both engines, and works on one of them. Every figure above is therefore reported with the conditions that would let a reader dismantle it, every correction runs in the text rather than a footnote, and the axis the employer loses is measured in the same detail as the axis it wins. That is the only arrangement under which such a comparison is worth anyone’s time — and the reader who concludes, from the five axes that go the other way, that this platform is the wrong choice for their workload will have used this study exactly as intended.

Conditions for M5 to M8 — the three-replica ring and the nested-document read
M7, M8 and M9 were taken on a six-node, two-datacentre ring, the working keyspace at three replicas in one datacentre, one replica per rack; nodes larger than the single-node host used for M1 to M6 (8 GiB with a 4 GiB heap against 3 GiB with a 2 GiB heap), which means end-to-end latencies across the two campaigns mix a replication effect with a hardware difference and must not be compared directly. M6 — The Data API's consistency levels are configuration, and they are asymmetric
Establishes, from the tier's own startup configuration: writes, ordinary reads and schema changes default to LOCAL_QUORUM; serial consistency to LOCAL_SERIAL; the lightweight-transaction retry count to three; and vector search to LOCAL_ONE. All are settable properties rather than fixed behaviour. The asymmetry between a quorum write and a single-replica vector read is the subject of M7.
M7 — Vector-search freshness under that asymmetry
Establishes: on the three-replica ring, with a sixteen-dimension cosine collection, the just-written vector was the top match on the first LOCAL_ONE query in 120 of 120 cycles — sixty idle, sixty under forty vector writes per second — and in no cycle did the quorum key read find the document while the vector search missed it. Insert-to-visible median 74 ms, HTTP-bound. Validity controls: the marker returned at cosine similarity 1.0 in rank one, background vectors at 0.83 or below, and an unrelated query returned a different top match.
Verdict: not detectable. This neither confirms nor refutes the searchability claim of section 5. It bounds the window below the measurement floor on a ring whose replicas share one host and answer in under a millisecond, and it names the wide-area topology this bench cannot reproduce as the place where the question is actually open.
M17 — Search freshness, both engines, one session
Establishes: writing a document and then querying for it through the search index, forty cycles each side on one host. The decomposing engine returned it on the first query in every cycle, at a median of 45 ms write-to-searchable, which bounds its index lag below one HTTP round trip without establishing that the lag is nil; its dispersion is wider than the comparator’s, at a ninety-fifth percentile of 85 ms and a maximum of 574. MongoDB’s $search required a median of thirty-four polling attempts and 1 015 ms; no cycle failed to become visible within the timeout. That figure was later shown to be an artefact of the probe and is superseded by measurement M18. The same documents were retrievable by identifier on MongoDB in 1.3 ms throughout, which isolates the lag to the search index rather than to the write.
Method: MongoDB 8.3.11 Community through the vendor’s packaged local image, bundling mongot 1.75.1 in its localDev edition (image digest sha256:e118f5c131c5004e4ccd0554d3f32b048b4af6a33325e80e424eaa1c8986d8f1, built 16 September 2026) — a generally-available-line version of the engine, in an edition the image labels as for local development; a search index was created and confirmed ready, and a query validated against it, before any measurement was taken.
Not established: the magnitude of the asynchronous lag on any other deployment — the image used is the vendor’s development and testing build, and a quantity that depends on the deployment cannot be vouched for by one of them; behaviour under load, the figure being an idle one, which would widen the lag rather than narrow it; the effect of replica count, the packaged image running a single member so that a majority write concern is satisfied trivially there; the index lag of the decomposing engine, which is bounded below the measurement floor rather than measured; and equivalence of the search paths, a vector index having been exercised on one side and a text index on the other, so that the comparison of ingestion architectures is an inference rather than an observation. What the two share, and what is being compared, is that one maintains its index on the write path and the other replicates to it asynchronously.

What the measurements do not establish
Any disk-bound behaviour: every regime was memtable-resident and no compaction was crossed. The separation of a per-field from a per-byte cost in the indexing term, which Part I now resolves in favour of bytes. The internal decomposition of the Data API tier's own cost, which Part I separates from storage but does not break down into parsing, shredding and serialisation. M18 — The asynchronous lag is a refresh interval, and the first figure overstated it
Establishes: inserting a random delay of up to 1 200 ms before each write, so that every phase of the indexing cycle is sampled rather than one, gives a lag spread from 89 ms to 1 170 ms with a mean of 646 and a standard deviation of 291 across sixty cycles. The signature is a periodic refresh of roughly a second, not a fixed pipeline delay.
What it corrects. The probe behind M17 wrote, polled until the document appeared, and wrote again — a loop that synchronises itself to the cycle, so that every write landed just after a commit and waited a full period. Its tight 1 015 ms was the worst phase of the cycle, and overstated the lag by about 1.6 times. The error was the measurer’s, found by his own follow-up rather than by an objection.
Consequence for the comparison: the mean gap against the synchronous index is about fourteen times rather than twenty-two, and the best-case gap about two — 89 ms against a 45 ms floor. The direction is unchanged and the margin is narrower.
Not established: the interval on any other deployment or edition. The engine measured is mongot 1.75.1 in its localDev edition; no configuration knob for the interval was found in the packaged image, so it is that edition’s internal default, and whether the managed service or a self-managed production edition commits on the same period is not known.
Raw record: findings_mongot_floor.json. — published in the repository, github.com/davidleconte/shredding-cost-hcd-mongodb
M19 — What the window costs, against how soon the read arrives
Establishes: sweeping the delay between the write and the search, thirty cycles at each of nine delays, both engines on one host. The decomposing engine missed no document at any delay, including zero. The asynchronous path missed every document at zero delay, 93 % at 100 ms, 83 % at 250, 50 % at 500, 33 % at 750, and none from 1 000 ms onward. The transition sits at the refresh interval measured in M18, and is located only to within a quarter of a second, the flanking points being 750 ms and 1 000 ms. Thirty cycles per point gives a confidence interval of roughly eighteen points around the middle values. The zero-delay row compares a native driver against an HTTP interface and therefore not the same instant; corrected for the forty-five millisecond difference it would read about 97 %.
What it withdraws. Earlier revisions of this part motivated the freshness argument with conversational retrieval — an assistant writing a fact and searching for it on the following turn. A turn driven by a language model takes seconds, so the window closes before the read arrives and the failure never occurs. That motivation is withdrawn. The advantage holds for writes composed with searches inside the refresh window, with no human and no model interposed. Which real workloads sit there is a question this study did not ask: no application’s write-to-search interval was measured, and naming candidate workloads — tool chains, ingestion loops — would repeat, one line later, the error this measurement just corrected.
Not established: the position of the transition on any other deployment, since it follows the refresh interval of this one; and whether any particular production workload sits below or above it, which is a property of the application rather than of either engine.

Limitations

Stated here rather than buried, because work that grades its claims owes the reader an account of where the grading came out badly. These limitations apply to the diptych as a whole.

Four substantive claims could not be verified against sources of acceptable rank, and three of the four concern the decomposing engine rather than its comparator: the enumeration of generic column names, the read-modify-write cycle, the consistency levels at which Data API writes execute, and the current general-availability status of MongoDB search per edition, since resolved from the vendor’s pages (section 1.3). The first three were subsequently measured and hold on the builds named; they carry the M marker, which is weaker than D: an observation of a build is not a vendor’s commitment. The fourth has since been resolved from the vendor’s own pages, and the paragraph above is corrected accordingly.

That the unverified residue falls almost entirely on one side is itself a finding, and it cuts against the side I work for. MongoDB’s mutation semantics, its index policy and its asynchronous search path are documented in version-pinned reference material and were confirmed verbatim. The equivalent statements about the Data API’s physical representation are not published anywhere I could reach. A reader may reasonably conclude that the system I know better is the one whose mechanism is harder to verify, and may weight the comparison accordingly. An earlier revision added that the asymmetry ran further still, nine measurements having been taken on one side and none on the other. That is no longer so: the comparator has since been measured on eight axes, and the results are in the tables of section 6. The documentary asymmetry above remains; the measurement asymmetry does not.

The write-cost measurement that Part I reports has since been resolved in one direction. A later campaign separated the two terms that the original run had grown together and found the cost carried by indexed bytes rather than by field count, at roughly 0.77 ms per indexed kilobyte. Readers who met an earlier revision of either part will have seen a per-field rate; it was withdrawn, and the correct unit is in Part I. Later campaigns separated where that cost is paid — a minority in the stateless tier, the majority in the stateful engine, the exact share unsettled between a tenth and a third — then found the rate’s boundary: it is a memtable-regime quantity that collapses behind a large fixed read cost once the cycle’s internal read leaves memory. They also measured the comparator at last: on the mutation axis the decomposing engine costs between roughly three and thirteen times a capability-matched MongoDB, the gap widening with indexed volume — a range of latencies, the slope ratios that circulate from this work being unsupported by anything in it. All of that is in Part I.

This study does not meet the standard it sets in section 4. Of the six requirements listed there, roughly three are met across the campaigns: distributions are reported, durability is mapped and declared, topology is declared. Load was driven closed-loop almost everywhere, one operation awaiting the reply to the last, which is the first thing that section tells a reader not to do; and only one campaign of seven left the cache. The directions reported in both parts survive that; the magnitudes are worth less than their decimal places suggest, and a reader should treat every figure here as an observation of one build on one machine rather than as a property of a product.

The axis on which this part rests was measured late, and on one deployment. An earlier revision recorded that it had never been measured at all, MongoDB’s search process appearing undeployable outside its managed service; the vendor’s own packaged local image made it available, and section 1.1 reports the result. The figure that matters — a periodic refresh averaging two thirds of a second, against none — rests on a single-member deployment, an idle system, a development edition of the engine (mongot 1.75.1, localDev), and two different search paths, a vector index against a text one. The architecture being compared is the same on both; the objects being searched are not. Load would widen the lag rather than narrow it, which means the reservation runs in the direction favourable to the conclusion, and a reader should discount accordingly rather than the reverse.

Every figure in this part was taken in a memtable-resident regime, and Part I has since shown that this is not a detail: the write-cost rate it reports collapses once the read leaves memory, so no quantity in either part should be read as a disk-regime figure. No SSTable was flushed and no compaction was crossed for any measurement reported here, so these are in-memory latencies. A disk-bound run at three replicas has since been executed for the write-cost measurements of Part I, where the indexed-content coefficient did not collapse; the freshness and consensus measurements of this part were not repeated there. A page-cache confound also survives in that later run, the operating system cache never having been dropped.

Two product pages could not be rendered during verification — the HCD 2.0 Data API limits page and the HCD 2.0 data-model page — so several product figures rest on the equivalent commercial documentation for the same API surface rather than on the exact edition under discussion. These substitutions are marked in the appendix entry concerned.

Everything concerning Cassandra 6.0 describes software in alpha, explicitly designated by its publishers as unsuitable for anything beyond development and testing. Its configuration surface, its syntax and its feature scope may all change before general availability.

Two sources were consulted through list mirrors rather than the Apache archive directly, and one through the project’s pull-request traffic rather than published documentation. Where that provenance bears on the weight of a claim, the claim carries the R marker rather than D — with one exception I would rather name than let a reader find. The alpha classification quoted in Part II is marked D although the only reachable copy of that announcement was a list mirror. The quotation is verbatim and the tag dates corroborate it from the project repository itself, but by the rule stated in this very paragraph it ought to carry R. Consistency is a property of a system of markers, not of any one of them, and this is where mine failed.

The interactive control in section 1 measures nothing. Its six index-lag values are plausible orders of magnitude chosen to span the space; they come from the author, not from any system, and the zero-lag setting corresponds to a behaviour this work could establish only below a detection floor of roughly one network round trip. It is a demonstrator of a mechanism, and the rule about diagrams carrying unearned authority applies to it with more force than to any static figure.
